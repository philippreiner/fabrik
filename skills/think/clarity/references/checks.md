# Checks

Start with the core set, add the set for the format, then add any other check the piece needs. That gives 22 to 24 checks; review against each. A check passes, or its trade-off is named in one line. Each line: the check, then how failure shows up.

## Point and ask

1. Buried lede: count the words before the point or the ask. Fails as: the ask after 100 words, or never.
2. The opening answers the three questions (what changed, why it matters, what you need from me) and orients a reader arriving cold. Fails as: an opening that describes the topic or the process, or one that jumps into instructions before the reader knows what they are about.
3. One point. Fails as: two candidate theses competing for the title.
4. The ask names who decides what, by when, and what happens if nobody acts. Fails as: "let me know your thoughts".
5. The decision state is explicit: made (recommendation plus the one number) or open (options plus criteria). Fails as: a recommendation dressed as options.
6. Missing facts are named, never invented. A gap the reader will certainly ask about (price, timing, data protection) is named on the page as open, with when it will be known; other gaps are listed below the piece with who can supply them. A fact found only in an unverified or AI-written draft counts as missing. Where the reader must set a value, a default is proposed and marked as a proposal. Fails as: a made-up figure, a gap the reader finds first, or "Open:" with no proposal.

## Value and stance

7. Someone could disagree with the core message (Duarte). Fails as: a topic where a point should be.
8. So-what pass: every section supports the point, and the cuts are listed. Fails as: slides kept for completeness.
9. Swap test: a competitor could not sign it unchanged. Fails as: "we help teams work better together".
10. Value (McEnerney): the reader group is named, and what they lose if they ignore this. Fails as: value asserted for "the business".
11. Vernunft test: each sentence has a reason a sensible person would accept. Fails as: adjectives doing the work of evidence.
12. Worst-light read (Basecamp): what would the most skeptical reader expect and not find? Fails as: surprises in the Q&A.
13. The number that justifies the recommendation is on the page, or its absence is named. Fails as: "significant savings".
14. Risks, costs and constraints (budget, legal, security, people) sit next to the recommendation. Cost, owner and where the data goes get one line each, or are named as open. A product that reads documents, connects to systems or records people needs a short data paragraph: what leaves the company, under which contract, who must approve. For European readers that covers data protection, and in Germany the works council whenever people's work or meetings are recorded. Fails as: "runs on EU infrastructure" as the only data line; a one-sided case, risks in an appendix, or a proposal with no price, owner or data line.
15. The reader knows why to trust the sender: team, track record, customers, method. Fails as: a pitch with nobody behind it, or product history where credentials belong.

## Concepts

16. Concept budget, set by this reader: at most one new term per sentence and two per slide or section, none stacked. Fails as: a sentence with three cones.
17. Every term new to this reader is translated at first use: plain meaning, why it matters. Fails as: the term used as if known, or letters to look up.
18. Nothing this reader already knows is translated or explained. Fails as: "run-rate, meaning annualized revenue" for a CFO; a sprint explained to an engineer.
19. One term per concept, no synonym rotation. Fails as: "customers / users / accounts / clients" for one group.
20. Numbers are in the reader's units: money, time, people, risk. Fails as: "5 GB", "p99 of 800 ms".
21. The failure movie is concrete: who sees what, and what it costs. Fails as: "less reliable", "suboptimal".
22. Each analogy maps one property, is followed by the precise claim, and says where it breaks. Fails as: an analogy carrying the argument.
23. A translation (English ↔ German) carries the concept, not the literal words, and the cones are recounted. Fails as: "Entscheidungsfindungsprozess" for "decision", or an English buzzword left standing in German.
24. Reader's frame first, your frame second; effects described from the reader's side (what they keep, do, get). Fails as: your architecture before their problem; "replaces none of your tools".

## Structure

25. The titles alone tell the story in order (horizontal logic). Fails as: Background, Approach, Results.
26. Each title is earned: the body proves exactly that claim, no more (vertical logic). Fails as: "pays off" above a small example, or a body that proves a different claim.
27. Reasons are the same kind, do not overlap, cover the question, and have an order. Fails as: three reasons because three felt right.
28. No contradictions between units, numbers or claims, including the piece's own limits against its features. Fails as: 12% on slide 3 and 15% on slide 9; "read-only access" next to "rewrites your tickets".
29. Evidence matches the claim it supports. Fails as: data about one use case cited for another.
30. Parallel items are parallel: same kind, similar length, same grammar. Fails as: a list mixing a goal, a task and a caveat.
31. Prose for reasoning; lists and tables for parallel items. Fails as: an argument chopped into bullets, or a comparison written as prose.
32. Context arrives when needed. Detail the decision does not need goes to an appendix, backup slide or speaker notes. Fails as: three slides of background before the point.
33. Familiar before new; define before compare. Fails as: a comparison of two things the reader cannot yet picture.
34. Options include doing nothing when that is real, and no option is built to lose. Fails as: a forced choice, or a strawman.
35. One idea per unit. Fails as: a slide with two charts and an "also".

## Sentences

36. Old information first, the point in the last words (Gopen & Swan). Fails as: the new number buried mid-sentence.
37. Subject and verb close together; the action in the verb, not a nominalization. Fails as: 15 words between "the team" and "decided"; "the implementation of", "eine Prüfung vornehmen".
38. Every action has someone doing it. Fails as: "mistakes were made", "es wurde entschieden".
39. Numbers instead of adjectives and vague amounts (Amazon), never invented. Fails as: "significant", "many", "deutlich".
40. The main clause comes before long conditions; no double negatives. Fails as: "If ..., and if ..., then ...", "not unlikely".
41. Ordinary words for ordinary things (Graham's talk test). Fails as: "utilize", "facilitate", "im Rahmen von".
42. Hedges locate the doubt or go. Punchy is not the same as absolute. Fails as: "may potentially", or certainty you do not have.
43. Sentence length varies on purpose: mostly 10 to 20 words, some short, some longer with a subordinate clause, none over about 30. Fails as: every sentence 8 to 14 words (the scan reports "uniform sentence length"), or a 40-word chain.
44. One idea per sentence; long sentences carry reasoning, never stacked attributes or trailing qualifiers. Fails as: "and", "which", "while" chaining three claims; "…, with the source for every finding" appended to a full sentence.
45. Concrete verbs and named nouns: cut, pay, ship, decide; the actual thing instead of "the facts", "the data", "this". Fails as: "address", "drive", "enable", "adressieren", or a reader asking "which facts?".

## Voice and AI tells (details in `voice.md`)

One run of `scripts/scan.py` covers most of 47, 48, 49 and 54; read its output instead of rereading the text for each.

46. The register fits a senior reader: their professional vocabulary, calm, no hype. Fails as: "get back on track" where the reader says "recovery"; "super", "game-changer".
47. No mirrored contrasts or antithesis pairs: "X, not Y", "not X but Y", "A does this; B does that". At most one per piece, and never in a title. Fails as: a title built on "nicht X, sondern Y".
48. No aphorisms, colon reveals or staged setups, and every paragraph, note and slide ends on the fact the reader needs next, never on a restating or witty closer. Fails as: "By the time X, Y", "The result:", a short quotable last line.
49. Rhythm varies: no fragment drumbeats, no three sentences or bullets opening with the same word, no product name as the subject of every other sentence. Fails as: "It flags… It flags… It drafts…", or the scan's "opens N of M sentences".
50. No meta talk or process in the output. Speaker notes are for the presenter, never to-dos. Fails as: "in this deck we will", "check the source before presenting".
51. The reader is trusted: no hand-holding. Fails as: "simply", "just", "as you know", or "obviously" hiding a skipped step.
52. Customers are quoted verbatim, with name and role. Fails as: paraphrased praise.
53. Every claim has a source or an owner. Fails as: "studies show", "the market expects".
54. AI filler is gone: run `scripts/scan.py`. Fails as: CUT hits, HIGHLIGHT clusters, flagged AI shapes.
55. Emphasis comes from content, not bold, exclamation marks or intensifiers. Fails as: three bold phrases per paragraph.
56. The piece says what is unknown instead of filling the gap with smooth sentences. Fails as: paragraphs with no facts in them.

## Form

57. Titles that carry the argument are full-sentence claims of at most two lines, and they vary in shape and length because they do different jobs (recommendation, fact, risk, number, decision). A reference slide (options, calculation, appendix) may carry a short label. Fails as: a topic label where a claim belongs, a three-line title, a slogan ("…before your competitor does"), two titles built on "X, because Y", or seven titles of the same length.
58. Glance test (Duarte): the point of a slide lands in about three seconds. Fails as: the eye wanders.
59. One chart, one message, a source under every number. Fails as: a dual-axis chart with four series.
60. Assertion-evidence (Alley): the body is visual evidence, not bullets restating the title. Fails as: title and bullets say the same thing.
61. One focal point per slide or tile; few type sizes. Fails as: everything the same size, or everything big.
62. Fit the medium: an email about 150 words unless a decision, owner, date or caveat would be lost; a slide under about 40 words. Fails as: a memo in an email body.
63. No eyebrows, kickers, taglines or helper text. Fails as: three lines of label above one claim.
64. It works without the presenter. Fails as: a slide that needs the voiceover.
65. At most one moment of delight per piece. Fails as: a joke on every slide.
